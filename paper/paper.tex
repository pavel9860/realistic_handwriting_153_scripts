\begin{abstract}
Document understanding needs labelled handwriting in every script it meets. Learned handwriting generators cover a
handful of scripts and need large per-script training sets; rendered handwriting fonts cover many scripts but look
mechanical. We present \textsf{hwsynth}, a training-free generator that writes any text, including inline
mathematics, chemistry and simple figures, in 153 of the 175 Unicode scripts. Letters are traced from handwriting
fonts into pen paths. A writer model with 44 genes separates identity from situation and adds personal letter
shapes and symbol habits. A movement model re-synthesises every stroke from lognormal sub-movements and trades shape
fidelity against jerk energy by an effort parameter. A tool model with 17 instruments deposits ink by nib shape,
ink class, speed and pressure, and a scan model ages and digitises the page. Twenty-four handwriting types can each
be written in ten styles and with either hand; in addition, personas are fitted by analysis-by-synthesis to real
writers of the IAM handwriting forms. A quality-diversity search fills a bank of realistic, stable and mutually
distinguishable individuals per type. Evaluation uses real lines split by form so that no test form is used for
fitting. \input{abstract_numbers}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\paragraph{The need.} Handwriting is a large share of the documents that machines are asked to read: filled
forms, letters, notes, prescriptions, exam sheets and archives. Text recognition, layout analysis and document
dewarping need it in quantity, with exact labels (characters, words, boxes, pen paths), in every script the system
will meet. Collecting and annotating real handwriting at that scale is slow and expensive, and for most of the
world's scripts there is little or none.

\paragraph{The gap.} Synthetic handwriting is the usual answer. The field has converged on learned generators:
recurrent networks for pen trajectories~\cite{graves2013}, GANs~\cite{fogel2020,kang2020},
transformers~\cite{bhunia2021,pippi2023} and diffusion models~\cite{nikolaidou2023,nikolaidou2024}
(survey:~\cite{diaz2025}). They are convincing \emph{for the scripts and styles they were trained on}, mostly Latin,
because they need thousands of labelled lines per script. For the other scripts practitioners render handwriting
fonts with random affine jitter. The result is recognisably mechanical: every instance of a letter is the same
glyph, strokes are uniform, baselines are straight, and the writing has no person behind it.

\paragraph{The question.} Can a generator built from explicit models of \emph{who} writes, \emph{how the hand
moves}, \emph{what tool touches the paper} and \emph{how the page is digitised} close that gap without a trained
network, in every script that fonts cover? Two difficulties follow. Each model must remove a specific source of the
mechanical look, and the dozens of interacting parameters must be set so that the output is realistic, stable and
diverse, which hand-tuning does not achieve.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item A letter source for 153 scripts: centrelines traced from 59 handwriting fonts and 158 Noto fonts, drawn per
grapheme cluster or per shaped word with correct bidirectional order (\S\ref{sec:letters}).
\item A writer model that separates identity (the discriminating elements of forensic
examination~\cite{huber1999}) from situation, with 24 types, 10 styles, either hand, personal letter shapes and
symbol habits (\S\ref{sec:writer}).
\item A movement model joining lognormal sub-movements~\cite{plamondon1995} with a minimum-jerk~\cite{flash1985}
effort trade-off that orders the styles from medical scrawl to calligraphy (\S\ref{sec:motion}).
\item Tool, ink and scan models for 17 instruments and aged, digitised paper (\S\ref{sec:tool}), and inline
mathematics, chemistry and figures (\S\ref{sec:math}).
\item Writer banks from a quality-diversity search and personas fitted to real writers (\S\ref{sec:banks}),
evaluated against real handwriting with form-disjoint splits (\S\ref{sec:refs}, \S\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Scope.} \textsf{hwsynth} produces offline images of handwriting with labels; it does not recognise
handwriting and does not imitate a specific person from a sample at inference time (personas are fitted
offline). Realism is measured with proxies against real scans; a perception study is future work
(\S\ref{sec:discussion}). Code, fonts manifest, banks, personas and this paper are released under CC BY-NC 4.0.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Learned handwriting generation.} Graves~\cite{graves2013} generated online trajectories with an LSTM and
a mixture density output. Haines et al.~\cite{haines2016} assembled a specific person's handwriting from their
glyphs. ScrabbleGAN~\cite{fogel2020} and GANwriting~\cite{kang2020} generate offline word images conditioned on
text and style; Handwriting Transformers~\cite{bhunia2021} and VATr~\cite{pippi2023} condition on a few style
images; latent diffusion~\cite{nikolaidou2023,nikolaidou2024} gives the most faithful styles today. All need
labelled handwriting of the target script and style. Our generator needs none and covers 153 scripts, at the cost
of the per-writer fidelity that learned models reach on their training scripts.

\paragraph{Fonts and perturbation.} Before deep generators, synthetic training data came from geometric
perturbation of real lines~\cite{varga2003} and from fonts. Fonts scale to every script with a font but repeat
glyphs exactly; we keep their coverage and redraw every glyph as a new movement by a person.

\paragraph{Models of handwriting movement.} The Kinematic Theory~\cite{plamondon1995,plamondon2006} represents a
stroke as a sum of lognormal velocity impulses; its Sigma-Lognormal model reconstructs real
trajectories~\cite{ferrer2020} and has been used to design them~\cite{berio2017}. Minimum-jerk
planning~\cite{flash1985} explains the smoothness of arm movements. We use the first for timing and corner cutting
and the second for the trade-off between effort and shape.

\paragraph{Individuality and writer identification.} Forensic examiners compare allograph design, connections,
proportions, slant, spacing, baseline, commencements and terminations, line quality and natural
variation~\cite{huber1999}; Srihari et al.~\cite{srihari2002} quantified individuality. Automatic writer
identification uses contour-direction and contour-hinge distributions~\cite{schomaker2004,bulacu2007} and path
signatures~\cite{lai2019,chen1957,lyons1998}. We use these features to measure and enforce that our types are
distinguishable and to fit personas to real writers.

\paragraph{Ink, paper and scans.} Franke and Rose~\cite{franke2004} related pen force and ink deposition for solid,
viscous and fluid inks; Sousa and Buchanan~\cite{sousa1999} modelled graphite on paper. Our tool and scan models
follow these classes as renderers, not physical simulations.

\paragraph{Evaluation and search.} We report Fr\'echet distances~\cite{heusel2017} and k-NN precision and
recall~\cite{kynkaanniemi2019} on ResNet18~\cite{he2016} embeddings and stroke features. The writer banks are filled
by MAP-Elites~\cite{mouret2015}, a quality-diversity algorithm related to novelty search~\cite{lehman2011}, with
iso-line variation~\cite{vassiliades2018}.

\section{System Overview}
\label{sec:overview}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the pipeline. Text is split into words, inline mathematics and drawing units.
Each unit's glyph centreline is reshaped by the writer and placed on a line; the hand moves the pen over the
strokes; the tool deposits ink; the page is composed on paper and optionally aged and scanned. Every stage is an
explicit, inspectable model with its parameters in one configuration module. Writers are gene vectors; the banks
of \S\ref{sec:banks} only select genes, no stage is learned. Each sample comes with word boxes, the pen paths and
the writer, tool and scan metadata.

\section{Letters in Every Script}
\label{sec:letters}

\paragraph{Scripts and units.} Each character gets its Unicode script~\cite{unicode}. Scripts with independent
letter forms (Latin, Cyrillic, Greek, Hebrew, Han, kana, Hangul, Ethiopic and 30 others) are drawn per grapheme
cluster, so every letter receives its own variation; all other scripts are shaped by HarfBuzz/Raqm and drawn per
word, so that Arabic joining and Indic conjuncts come from the font. Right-to-left runs are reversed while digits
and Latin runs inside them keep their order. Scripts without spaces are split into short chunks (single characters
for Han and kana), keeping closing punctuation with the preceding chunk.

\paragraph{Fonts.} A manifest lists 59 handwriting fonts (SIL OFL or Apache) with class labels (cursive, print,
brush), script restrictions and preferences (e.g.\ 80\,\% of Hebrew writers use a cursive Hebrew font), and 158
Noto Sans fonts as fallback. A font serves a script when it covers 90\,\% of the script's core letters (base
letters without marks or compatibility forms; explicit alphabets for Latin, Cyrillic, Greek, Arabic, Han, kana,
Hangul and Syriac). A writer uses its Latin font for every script that font serves and otherwise a handwriting
font of the script; sixteen fonts with a childish design are reserved for the child type and the uneducated
style. Coverage: 153 scripts, 15 of them with handwriting fonts.

\paragraph{Centrelines.} A glyph is rasterised at 160\,px per em, skeletonised and traced into a graph: free spurs
shorter than 5\,\% of the em are pruned, junction clusters merged, isolated dots kept, and edges continued through
junctions along the smallest turn, which yields pen strokes in a natural order. Glyph size is normalised per script
by the height of a reference letter in x-heights (e.g.\ Han twice the Latin x-height).

\section{Writers}
\label{sec:writer}

\paragraph{Genes.} A writer is a vector of 44 genes in $[0,1]$, decoded to natural units (Appendix,
Table~\ref{tab:genes}). Twenty-four are \emph{identity} genes after the discriminating elements of~\cite{huber1999}:
size, width, ascender, descender and capital proportions, slant, letter and word spacing, line pitch, roundness and
angularity (garland, arcade, angular and thread movement), joining, entry hooks and exit flicks, dot placement and
form, pressure, the allograph set (font) and the share of letters in a second allograph, personal letter shapes,
handedness, capitals-only writing and zone flattening. The others are \emph{situational}: speed, effort, letter
jitter, baseline wander, tremor, decay towards word ends, cross-outs and retracing.

\paragraph{Types and individuals.} A type fixes a centre for every identity gene: listed values grounded in a
description (Appendix, Table~\ref{tab:types}) and, for the rest, fixed values drawn from the type name, so that
each type has one recognisable hand including its font. An individual draws each centred gene within $\pm12\,\%$ of
its range. Genes the type leaves free are drawn from skewed priors: noise genes as $u^k$ ($k=2$--$4$), signed drifts
near zero, rare events near zero. Uniform priors, used first, gave the median writer half of the maximum jitter on
every channel at once, which looked unstable and childish; adults vary narrowly to moderately. The 24 types range
from school cursive, copperplate and thread-like scripts through print, block capitals and micro writing to elderly,
child and four rounded hands (rounded neat, bubbly print, looped cursive, soft upright).

\paragraph{Styles.} A style is a situation applied to a person: situational genes are remapped into style ranges,
keeping the person's relative position in each range, and a few identity genes are shifted (hurried writers join
more and slant further). Ten styles are defined: casual, formal, accurate, calligraphic, professional, hurried,
medical, educated, uneducated and unsupported (a sheet held in the hand). Each style also sets a tool prior and
the paper.

\paragraph{Handedness.} About 10\,\% of writers are left-handed (and all of the left-hooked type). They pull
horizontal strokes from right to left, draw some loops clockwise, write more upright, hold broad nibs steeper and
smear wet ink in the writing direction.

\paragraph{Natural variation.} Repeated letters are never identical: neighbouring letters share correlated
size, slant and baseline jitter (AR(1), $\rho=0.6$); stroke ends overshoot or stop short; ink flow drifts from
stroke to stroke; a fixed share of letters is written in a second allograph. Personal letter shapes give each
writer its own version of every letter: a smooth deformation and a width, height and slant tweak drawn once per
writer and letter from its identity, so that all instances of the writer's \emph{a} share them while another
writer's \emph{a} differs. The identity is derived from genes that no style changes, so a person keeps its letters
in every style.

\paragraph{Symbol habits.} Writers differ in how they write symbols, which forensic examiners also compare. Each
writer has habits drawn once from its identity, with probabilities set per type: crossed sevens and crossed
\emph{z}, slashed zeros, flagged ones, a quotation-mark style (English curly, straight, German low--high or French guillemets)
and the plus sign for the ampersand. European cursive types cross sevens and \emph{z} more often, drafting types
slash zeros.

\section{Hand Movement}
\label{sec:motion}

\paragraph{Lognormal sub-movements.} Each stroke is reduced to virtual targets (Douglas--Peucker turning points).
Segment $j$ becomes a sub-movement with velocity $\mathbf{D}_j\Lambda(t;t_{0j},\mu_j,\sigma_j)$, where $\Lambda$ is
the lognormal impulse response of the Kinematic Theory~\cite{plamondon1995}. Starts are spaced by
$2\,o\,e^{\mu-\sigma^2}$, twice the overlap ratio $o$ times the time of peak speed (isochrony). Fast, fluent
writers overlap more and cut corners; slow, careful writers and discrete-stroke scripts (Han, kana, Hangul,
Devanagari) reach every target. The speed of the synthesised movement drives the tool.

\paragraph{Effort and jerk.} The integrated path is then the solution of
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\mathbf{x}}\;\lambda(\epsilon)\,\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{p}\rVert^2+\lVert D_3\mathbf{x}\rVert^2,
  \qquad \log_{10}\lambda=-6+6.5\,\epsilon,
\end{equation}
with $D_3$ the third difference (jerk) and end points held: a writer with low effort $\epsilon$ follows the
minimum-jerk path and lets the shape go; with high effort the shape is kept at the cost of jerk energy. Effort is a
situational gene whose style ranges rise from medical (0--0.12) through hurried, professional, casual, educated and
formal to accurate and calligraphic (0.9--1).

\paragraph{Lines.} Lines follow wrist-pivot arcs that reset at hand shifts every 25--60\,mm, slow random baseline
wander (correlation length 15--60\,mm), an optional bow and slope, all damped on ruled paper; involuntary tremor
is added last.

\section{Tools, Ink, Paper and Scan}
\label{sec:tool}

\paragraph{Tools.} Seventeen tools combine a nib shape with an ink class~\cite{franke2004} (Appendix,
Table~\ref{tab:tools}). Round nibs set width from the tip; broad nibs give the thick--thin contrast
$w+e\lvert\sin(\phi+\alpha)\rvert$ for stroke direction $\phi$ and nib angle $\alpha$; brushes widen with
pressure$^{1.5}$ and taper. \emph{Solid} ink (graphite) follows force and shows paper grain; \emph{viscous} ink
(ballpoint paste) thins with speed, skips on fast strokes and blobs where the pen lands; \emph{fluid} inks (gel,
rollerball, fineliner, fountain, markers) keep their density, pool at slow points, bleed and darken where strokes
cross. Pressure combines the writer's push, its variation, extra force on downstrokes and lift-off ramps. Strokes
are drawn as dense anti-aliased discs at $3\times$ supersampling; the x-height is at least four nib widths.

\paragraph{Paper and scan.} Paper has a tint, fibre noise and optional ruling that the writer follows. The scan
model ages and digitises the page: yellowing and foxing with document age (Beta-distributed, mostly recent), ink
fading, show-through of the reverse side, uneven light, greyscale or (rarely) bitonal conversion with an adaptive
threshold, gamma, noise, blur, dust and JPEG compression. It is applied to half of all samples by default.

\section{Mathematics, Chemistry and Figures}
\label{sec:math}

Inline \verb|$...$| segments use a small TeX-like syntax: superscripts and subscripts, fractions, roots, Greek
letters and symbols, drawn arrows, \verb|\ce{...}| chemical equations (counts become subscripts, charges
superscripts, reaction and equilibrium arrows) and \verb|\benzene|. They are laid out as boxes of pen strokes: glyph
runs come from the writer; rules, radicals, arrows and rings are drawn as strokes; the hand then moves over the
whole expression like a word, so mathematics and chemistry match the surrounding writing. Figures (circle,
ellipse, triangle, square, rectangle, arrow, star, tick, cross, axes with a curve, underline) are planned as
freehand paths that do not quite close, overshoot at corners and bow slightly, and are moved and inked by the
same models.

\section{Real References and Leakage Control}
\label{sec:refs}

\paragraph{IAM handwriting forms (primary).} The handwritten part of 1\,535 IAM forms~\cite{marti2002} (one writer
per form, clean scans, English) is cut into text lines (row-profile bands after removing rulings) at 150\,dpi,
giving \input{iam_numbers} The copy available to us has no writer identifiers, so the split is by form group:
forms with the same text (e.g.\ a01-000, a01-000u, a01-000x) are always on the same side, and 30\,\% of the groups
are held out as the test split. Writers who wrote several groups may appear on both sides; this is the one
leakage we cannot exclude.

\paragraph{Handwritten web documents (secondary).} Lines of 980 handwritten documents collected from the web,
labelled by the language tag of their file names, give script targets beyond Latin (Cyrillic, Japanese, Hebrew,
Greek, Arabic; a few documents each). They may contain printed lines and are used only for the per-script targets
of Figure~\ref{fig:map}.

\paragraph{Stability from real handwriting.} Baseline instability is the standard deviation of word ink centres
around their fitted line, in x-heights (from the dense core band of the row profile). On clean real lines it
runs from about 0.13 (steady) to 0.35 (wavy); higher values come from extraction errors. Real and generated lines
are measured with the same function.

\paragraph{Leakage rules.} Search and persona fitting use only the fit split; metrics use only the test split.
Search, fitting and metrics use different texts. Bank admission is re-evaluated with fresh seeds. Realism
statistics are normalised leave-one-document-out.

\section{Writer Banks and Personas}
\label{sec:banks}

\paragraph{Quality-diversity search.} Each type has a MAP-Elites archive~\cite{mouret2015} over two measured
behaviour descriptors: the slant of near-vertical pen-path segments (7 bins) and pen lifts per letter (5 bins).
An individual is rendered on four search texts, in the casual style and a random other style, and scored by
\begin{equation}
  Q=\textit{realism}-4\max(0,\,\textit{instability}-0.15)-[\textit{separation}\le 0].
\end{equation}
\emph{Realism} is the mean of two z-scores: the cosine similarity of the line embeddings to their five nearest fit
lines, and minus the distance of the standardised stroke features to their five nearest fit lines. Each is
standardised by the same statistic of real fit lines against other documents. \emph{Separation} is the distance
to the nearest other type's centroid minus the distance to the own centroid, in contour-hinge space~\cite{bulacu2007}
($\chi^2$) and path-signature space~\cite{lai2019}. New individuals are iso-line variations~\cite{vassiliades2018}
of two elites of the same type within the type's range. Elites are banked when a re-evaluation with two fresh
seeds keeps realism at or above the median of all re-evaluated elites, separation $>0$ and instability $\le 0.35$.
The threshold is relative because realism is a z-score against the reference set: against the homogeneous IAM forms
even good writers score below the real mean, and a fixed threshold calibrated on another reference rejects all.

\paragraph{Personas fitted to real writers.} For each fit-split IAM form an evolution strategy (10 random starts of
random types, 5 generations of 8 children, step size decaying from 0.12) searches 22 genes so that two generated
lines match the writer: $\chi^2$ distance of the contour-hinge distributions plus $0.02$ times the mean squared
difference of standardised stroke features, both averaged over the writer's lines. Each persona keeps its form,
distance and nearest type, whose font class and symbol habits it uses. The fitting is resumable: every persona is
saved when it is found. \input{persona_numbers}

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\paragraph{Protocol.} All metrics are computed on the IAM test split with texts disjoint from the search and
fitting texts. Systems: rendering a handwriting font (\emph{font}); uniform random genes (\emph{random}); type
priors (\emph{prior}); the searched bank (\emph{bank}); the fitted personas (\emph{personas}); and the bank without
the scan, pen, variation or joining models. Measures: realism (\S\ref{sec:banks}); Fr\'echet distance (FD) of
ResNet18 embeddings and of stroke features~\cite{heusel2017}; k-NN precision and recall~\cite{kynkaanniemi2019};
diversity (mean pairwise cosine distance of writer embeddings); writer identifiability (1-NN over lines);
character error rate of Tesseract~\cite{smith2007} on English lines; time per line. The reference row compares
real test lines of one half of the forms with the other half.

\paragraph{The path from mechanical to real.} Figure~\ref{fig:map} places real lines and generated lines in one
plane: the first two principal components of real lines only (embeddings with standardised stroke features). Font
rendering lies far from every real cloud. Each model moves the styles towards the real Latin cloud; the largest

searched bank and the fitted personas, move the styles further towards the centre of the IAM cloud. Calligraphy
stays apart, as broad-nib contrast is rare in everyday handwriting. Text in other scripts leaves the font position
and moves into the general handwriting region, but not reliably towards its own small real cloud (Arabic moves
away from its fifteen real lines): the plane is dominated by Latin handwriting and the non-Latin targets are too
small and too mixed to show script-specific convergence. Figure~\ref{fig:progression} shows the stages with the
realism score: the writer stage is the large step, after which the score changes little.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the measures can and cannot say.} Realism is a proxy: ImageNet embeddings and 22 stroke statistics
see texture, layout and stroke statistics, not whether letterforms are correct for a script, and the z-score treats
scanner noise as atypical of the clean IAM scans, so the scan model improves FD and recall while lowering realism. FD and
recall are the more informative measures for coverage of real handwriting. No measure replaces a perception study,
where people try to tell real from generated lines.

\paragraph{References.} The IAM copy has no writer identifiers, so test forms may share writers with fit forms.
IAM is English and clean; the web documents add scripts but few documents each and possibly printed lines, so the
non-Latin targets of Figure~\ref{fig:map} are indicative.

\paragraph{Generator.} Scripts without a handwriting font inherit Noto letterforms, which the writer and movement
models make handwritten in execution but not in allograph design. Personas reproduce the statistics of a real
writer's handwriting, not its exact letterforms. The banks are small after the strict re-evaluation. Generation
takes 0.3--0.5\,s per line on one CPU core.

\paragraph{Next steps.} A perception study; handwriting fonts for more scripts; real references with writer
identifiers and more scripts; page-level phenomena (margins, insertions, marginalia); learned realism judges; and
using \textsf{hwsynth} as a teacher for learned generators in scripts without real data.

% ===============================================================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Explicit models of the writer, the hand, the tool and the scan, set by a leakage-controlled quality-diversity
search and fitted to real writers, produce natural, stable and distinguishable handwriting in 153 scripts, with
mathematics, chemistry and figures, without any trained generator. Every layer is inspectable and controllable:
one can ask for a left-handed elderly writer in a hurry, with a pencil, on an old scanned page.
